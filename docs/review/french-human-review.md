⚠ Changed in machine review: Added: être → ét-; -ger/-cer spelling only before a.

### Subjunctive Present: Regular Formation and Core Triggers `subjonctif-present-formation` (B2)

⚠ Changed in machine review: Corrected: que alone does not mark the subjunctive.

**Explanation.** The present subjunctive is formed by taking the ils/elles form of the present tense, dropping -ent, and adding subjunctive endings: -e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent. Crucially, the nous and vous forms borrow from the imparfait stem instead, so que nous parlions looks like the imparfait but is actually subjunctive. The subjunctive itself is not a tense but a mood: it appears in a subordinate clause introduced by que, after verbs and expressions of necessity, wish, emotion, or doubt (il faut que, je veux que, je suis content que). Recognising these everyday trigger expressions is often more useful for reading than memorising every form, since the subjunctive rarely changes an English translation.

**Pattern.** parler (ils parlent) -> que je parle, que tu parles, qu'il parle, que nous parlions, que vous parliez, qu'ils parlent

**Common mistake.** The nous/vous subjunctive forms (parlions, parliez) look identical to the imparfait for -er verbs. que on its own does not decide which it is, because que also introduces ordinary indicative clauses (je pense qu'il vient). What decides is the trigger: il faut que nous parlions is subjunctive; nous parlions hier is imparfait.

### Subjunctive Present: être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir, savoir, vouloir `subjonctif-present-irregulars` (B2)

**Explanation.** A small set of very common verbs have irregular subjunctive stems that must be learned individually: être (que je sois), avoir (que j'aie), aller (que j'aille), faire (que je fasse), pouvoir (que je puisse), savoir (que je sache), and vouloir (que je veuille). Some of these, like faire and pouvoir, still take regular subjunctive endings on an irregular stem; être and avoir are irregular in both stem and endings. Because these seven verbs are extremely frequent, they appear constantly after subjunctive triggers in everyday conversation, so recognising them on sight matters more than being able to produce every form.

**Pattern.** être -> que je sois, que nous soyons; avoir -> que j'aie, que nous ayons

**Common mistake.** que nous soyons/ayons and que vous soyez/ayez don't follow either the regular subjunctive pattern or the irregular singular stems - they must be learned as their own forms.

### Plus-que-parfait: The Past Before the Past `plus-que-parfait` (B2)

**Explanation.** The plus-que-parfait (pluperfect) describes an action that was already completed before another past moment. It is built exactly like the passé composé, but with avoir or être in the imparfait instead of the present: il avait annoncé (he had announced), elle était partie (she had left). The same auxiliary choice and past-participle agreement rules from the passé composé carry over unchanged. In storytelling and everyday conversation, the plus-que-parfait lets a sentence step backward in time to explain the background to an event that has already been described in the passé composé or imparfait.

**Pattern.** avoir/être (imparfait) + past participle: il avait annoncé, elle était partie

**Common mistake.** The plus-que-parfait doesn't move the story forward the way the passé composé does; it explains what had already happened before the main past event.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon frère avait déjà mangé quand je suis rentré. | My brother had already eaten when I got home. | avait + participle: an earlier completed event | ☐ | |
| Elle était partie avant que nous arrivions à la fête. | She had left before we arrived at the party. | être + participle, agreement: partie | ☐ | |
| Ils n'avaient jamais vu une si grande maison. | They had never seen such a big house. | avaient + participle: background detail | ☐ | |

### Passé Simple: Recognising the Storytelling Past `passe-simple` (B2)

**Explanation.** The passé simple is a literary past tense used almost exclusively in writing - storybooks, novels, and historical accounts - never in everyday speech, where the passé composé takes its place. Regular verbs form it fairly predictably: -er verbs take -ai, -as, -a, -âmes, -âtes, -èrent; -ir/-re verbs mostly take -is, -is, -it, -îmes, -îtes, -irent. A handful of very common irregular verbs have their own passé simple stems that are worth recognising by sight: il fut (être), il eut (avoir), il vint (venir), il fit (faire), il dit (dire). Because this app's C1/C2 texts include exactly this kind of narrative prose, the goal here is recognition - matching a passé simple form back to its infinitive - not production.

**Pattern.** parler -> il parla; finir -> il finit; être -> il fut; avoir -> il eut; faire -> il fit; venir -> il vint

**Common mistake.** Don't try to use the passé simple yourself in conversation or everyday writing - it survives only in narrative prose. Your job is to recognise it, not produce it.

| Le petit garçon prit son sac et partit à l'école. | The little boy took his bag and left for school. | prendre, passé simple: prit | ☐ | |

### Si-Clauses I: Real Conditions `si-clauses-real` (B2)

⚠ Changed in machine review: Scoped: no future after si only when si = if (condition), not whether.

**Explanation.** The simplest si-clause pattern pairs si + present tense in the condition with a present or future tense in the result: si tu ranges ta chambre, tu peux sortir (if you tidy your room, you can go out) or si tu ranges ta chambre, tu pourras sortir (...you'll be able to go out). This describes something realistic or likely, not a hypothetical - it's the most common si-pattern in everyday plans and their consequences. Never put a future tense directly after si itself; the future belongs only in the result clause.

**Pattern.** si + present, ... present/futur: si tu ranges ta chambre, tu pourras sortir

**Common mistake.** When si means if and introduces a condition, its verb is never in the future or conditional, even when the result clause is: si le vote passe (not si le vote passera). This does not apply when si means whether: je me demande s'il viendra.

### Si-Clauses II: Present Hypotheticals `si-clauses-hypothetical` (B2)

**Explanation.** The second si-pattern pairs si + imparfait with a conditionnel result: si nous avions plus de temps, nous voyagerions davantage (if we had more time, we would travel more). Unlike the real-condition pattern, this describes something imagined or contrary to the current facts - we don't currently have more time. This pairing is extremely common in everyday conversation, when someone floats a hypothetical scenario about their current situation. As with the real-condition pattern, the imparfait after si is fixed - never the conditionnel itself.

**Pattern.** si + imparfait, ... conditionnel: si nous avions plus de temps, nous voyagerions davantage

**Common mistake.** Never put the conditionnel directly after si: si nous avions (not si nous aurions).

### Present Participle and Gerund: en + -ant `participe-present-gerondif` (B2)

⚠ Changed in machine review: Corrected: invariant participle vs agreeing -ant adjective; étant/ayant/sachant.

**Explanation.** The present participle is formed from the nous form of the present tense: nous parlons -> parlant, nous finissons -> finissant. On its own it describes an accompanying action and never agrees: une décision surprenant tout le monde. Many -ant words also exist as ordinary adjectives, which do agree: une décision surprenante (a surprising decision). Three common verbs have irregular forms: être -> étant, avoir -> ayant, savoir -> sachant. Preceded by en, it becomes the gerund and expresses two actions happening at the same time, or how/while something is done: il a répondu en souriant (he answered while smiling/smiling as he answered). This construction is common in everyday spoken and written French for compressing two related actions into one sentence, so recognising en + -ant as simultaneous action - not a separate clause - speeds up reading.

**Pattern.** nous parlons -> (en) parlant; nous finissons -> (en) finissant

**Common mistake.** en + -ant means while/by doing something at the same time, not a separate new sentence - don't look for a second subject.

### The Passive Voice `voix-passive` (B2)

⚠ Changed in machine review: Added: passive vs state (la porte est fermée).

**Explanation.** The passive voice is formed with être plus a past participle, and the participle agrees with the subject like an adjective: le gâteau a été préparé (the cake was made), les invitations ont été envoyées (the invitations were sent). The person or thing actually doing the action, if mentioned, follows par: le gâteau a été préparé par ma grand-mère. Everyday French uses the passive to foreground the event itself over who did it, especially when the person responsible isn't known or doesn't matter, so a sentence with être + participle + par is very often passive, not just a normal être-sentence. Without par, être + participle can also simply describe a state: la porte est fermée (the door is closed) says how the door is, not that someone is closing it.

**Pattern.** être (any tense) + past participle (+ par + agent): le gâteau est préparé; le gâteau a été préparé par ma grand-mère

**Common mistake.** Don't confuse a passive construction (le gâteau a été préparé) with a normal passé composé using être as the auxiliary for a movement verb (elle est partie) - check whether the participle is acting like an adjective describing the subject receiving an action.

| Example (French) | English | Note | OK | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### Subjunctive After Conjunctions: bien que, avant que, pour que, à moins que `subjonctif-after-conjunctions` (C1)

**Explanation.** Beyond verbs like vouloir or douter, a fixed set of conjunctions always trigger the subjunctive in the clause that follows: bien que/quoique (although), avant que (before), pour que/afin que (so that), à moins que (unless), sans que (without), jusqu'à ce que (until). These conjunctions introduce a subordinate idea that is conceded, anticipated, or not yet realised, which is exactly the kind of meaning the subjunctive marks. In everyday conversation, bien que is especially common as a way to concede a point before adding a but.

**Pattern.** bien que + subjunctive: bien qu'il pleuve, nous sortirons

**Common mistake.** Don't confuse bien que (although, + subjunctive) with parce que (because, + indicative) - they look similar but trigger opposite moods.

**Explanation.** The third si-pattern pairs si + plus-que-parfait with a conditionnel passé result: si nous avions réservé plus tôt, les billets auraient coûté moins cher (if we had booked earlier, the tickets would have cost less). ### Future Perfect: What Will Have Happened `futur-anterieur` (C1)

**Explanation.** The futur antérieur describes an action that will be finished before a future point in time: quand il aura terminé, il partira (when he has finished, he will leave). It is formed exactly like the passé composé, but with avoir or être in the futur simple: il aura terminé, elle sera partie. In everyday planning, it often follows quand, dès que, or après que to sequence two future events precisely - the completed one first, in the futur antérieur, and the following one in the futur simple.

**Pattern.** avoir/être (futur simple) + past participle: il aura terminé, elle sera partie

**Common mistake.** After quand/dès que with a future meaning, French requires a future tense (often the futur antérieur), where English often uses a present: quand il aura fini (not quand il a fini) = when he has finished.

### Past Conditional: Would Have Done `conditionnel-passe` (C1)

**Explanation.** The conditionnel passé is formed with avoir or être in the conditionnel plus a past participle: il aurait fini (he would have finished), elle serait partie (she would have left). Its main job is completing a past hypothetical after a si + plus-que-parfait clause, expressing what would have happened under different circumstances. It follows the same auxiliary-choice and agreement rules as the passé composé.

**Pattern.** avoir/être (conditionnel) + past participle: il aurait fini, elle serait partie

**Common mistake.** Don't mix up the conditionnel passé (aurait fini) with the plus-que-parfait (avait fini) - only the auxiliary's tense changes, but the meaning shifts from a simple past fact to a hypothetical would have.

### Reported Speech: Tense-Shifting (present -> imparfait, futur -> conditionnel) `reported-speech` (C1)

⚠ Changed in machine review: Added: still-true statements may keep their tense.

**Explanation.** When a reporting verb (dire, expliquer, annoncer...) is in the past, the tense of what's reported usually shifts back one step, exactly the way English backshifts said would for said will. Present becomes imparfait: elle a dit qu'elle était fatiguée (she said she was tired, from je suis fatiguée). Futur simple becomes conditionnel: il a annoncé qu'il arriverait bientôt (he announced he would arrive soon, from j'arriverai bientôt). Passé composé becomes plus-que-parfait: elle a expliqué qu'elle avait déjà mangé (she explained she had already eaten, from j'ai déjà mangé). This tense-shift only applies when the reporting verb is in a past tense; if the reporting verb is in the present, no shift happens: elle dit qu'elle est fatiguée keeps the present. When what was said is still true as you report it, the original tense can also stay: il m'a dit qu'il habite à Lyon.

**Pattern.** reporting verb in the past + present -> imparfait; futur -> conditionnel; passé composé -> plus-que-parfait

**Common mistake.** Don't backshift the tense if the reporting verb itself is in the present - elle dit qu'elle est fatiguée stays in the present; the shift only happens after a past reporting verb.

| Example (French) | English | Note | OK | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon frère a dit qu'il était fatigué après le match. | My brother said he was tired after the match. | present (est fatigué) -> imparfait (était fatigué) | ☐ | |
| Elle a promis qu'elle m'aiderait pour mes devoirs. | She promised she would help me with my homework. | futur (aidera) -> conditionnel (aiderait) | ☐ | |
| Il a expliqué qu'il avait déjà rangé sa chambre. | He explained he had already tidied his room. | passé composé (ai rangé) -> plus-que-parfait (avais rangé) | ☐ | |
